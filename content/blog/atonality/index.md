The last months have been… not great for my level of cognitive dissonance. We are clearly at the start of the singularity, however defined. Riemann, Navier-Stokes, you know the drill, and the labs might be sitting on cryptography results that would have huge bearing on my entire field, not to mention general society and its technological infrastructure. If not yet, at this rate they probably will, soon. But I spend my days touching grass, i.e. going to work and living my private life as if nothing changed.

I'm not sure if I can integrate this. Arbitraging AGI-pilledness between the Twitter AI bubble and my sleepy Uni town has been good for my relative status, but did not require me to truly act like I mean it, only to show the METR plot every few months, and to use AI tools more often (and more competently) than my coworkers. I looked at straight lines on graphs, nodded along to Dwarkesh, and then went into work doing basically the same thing as everyone else, only four to six months earlier. I'm not sure if I have it in me to stop saving for retirement and start doing psychedelics each week.

But it is going to be hard to just keep going like this. Obviously, we are way past the point of me making any useful contribution to calculations, simulations and theory work. That had never been my strong suit, and Opus 4.5 was the last frontier model I might have competed with, but I'm exactly in the same boat as many junior math and CS colleagues. In the lab, I'll have comparative advantage against humanoid robots for at least long enough to finish my PhD comfortably. The system has enough inertia to even allow me to produce something that would have looked very impressive a few years ago, and at least respectable now, whether or not I think any of the results matter. And then I will enter the job market to do… what exactly?

Many people who recognize what is coming are now associated with the AI-industrial complex or its direct opponents, and many have acted on the implications of their worldview. Some may work at METR or Redwood. Some may have quit their cushy professorships to become full-time existential risk alarmists. Some may have quit their cushy professorships to speed up the creation of the machine god.  Most of them probably are doing what they think is important to do. I am telling chatbots to blow up two pages of details into a 20 page project proposal, and then to reduce them again into a one-page summary[^1]. 

If I follow my expertly crafted plans, I am going to spend my next 12 months building some quantum optics experiments with incremental improvements on the SOTA, but no fundamental knowledge gain, and writing a run-of-the-mill publication based on the numerical analyses that Claude conjures out of the data. Afterwards, I will spend half a year to write a PhD dissertation that will be read by, like, 10 people. And of which many people will assume that Claude will have written it for me, even if I would never do that[^2]. Because who would choose to write this stuff manually? Previously, I was able to tell myself that I was acquiring rare skills, healthy personal habits, and those sweet, prestigious two letters in front of my name, all of which would be useful later on. But are they going to be, really? What is the opportunity cost here?

Of course, this is all very privileged stuff to be crying about. Late last year, I was at a local AI workshop where a woman described herself as being a part-time marketing copy writer, and part-time Polish-German translator. She wanted to know whether her jobs were in danger. She sounded appropriately depressed. I didn't have the heart to speak to her. I most likely will have a salary for the next year, and the freedom to write sad blog posts during work hours, if I feel like it. 

But I don't know where to go from here. I don't know whether there is even a correct direction to go, any consonance to find. Just that the best available move can't be carrying on as if nothing is happening. It can't be.
